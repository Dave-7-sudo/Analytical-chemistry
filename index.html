1. 📏 CALIBRATION

📖 DEFINITION

Calibration is the process of establishing a relationship between measurements obtained from an instrument and known reference standards to ensure accuracy and reliability of results.

✨ KEY POINTS

  • Compares instrument readings to known standard values
  • Corrects systematic errors in measurements
  • Must be performed regularly to maintain accuracy
  • Uses certified reference materials

🎯 PURPOSE

  • ✓ Ensures instrument gives correct readings
  • ✓ Minimizes measurement errors
  • ✓ Establishes traceability to standards
  • ✓ Required for quality assurance

💡 EXAMPLES

  • → Calibrating a pH meter with buffer solutions (pH 4, 7, 10)
  • → Calibrating analytical balance with standard weights
  • → Calibrating spectrophotometer with standard solutions
  • → Calibrating burette by measuring delivered volumes gravimetrically

📊 CALIBRATION CURVE

• Graph plotting instrument response vs. known concentrations

• Used to determine unknown concentrations from instrument readings

• Should be linear (straight line) for most analyses

Equation: y = mx + c (Beer's Law for spectrophotometry)

2. 🧪 STANDARD SOLUTION

📖 DEFINITION

A standard solution is a solution whose concentration is accurately known.

🔬 TYPES OF STANDARD SOLUTIONS

✅ PRIMARY STANDARD SOLUTION

  • Made by dissolving exact amount of primary standard substance
  • Concentration calculated directly from mass and volume
  • No need for standardization
  • Example: Dissolving 5.305g Na₂CO₃ in 1L water → 0.05M solution

⚠️ SECONDARY STANDARD SOLUTION

  • Prepared approximately, then standardized
  • Concentration determined by titration against primary standard
  • Used when primary standard not available
  • Examples: HCl, NaOH, KMnO₄ solutions

🧮 CONCENTRATION FORMULA

Concentration (M) = (mass of solute / molar mass) / volume in liters

🏪 STORAGE

  • Use clean, dry bottles
  • Label clearly with concentration and date
  • Store in cool, dark place (for light-sensitive solutions)
  • Some solutions have limited shelf life

3. ⚗️ HOW TO PREPARE A STANDARD SOLUTION ⭐ NEW!

Complete step-by-step procedure for preparing standard solutions!

🛠️ EQUIPMENT NEEDED

  • ✓ Analytical balance (4 decimal places)
  • ✓ Volumetric flask (appropriate size)
  • ✓ Beaker (for dissolving)
  • ✓ Glass rod (for stirring)
  • ✓ Wash bottle with distilled water
  • ✓ Funnel (for transfer)
  • ✓ Weighing bottle or watch glass
  • ✓ Primary standard substance

CALCULATE MASS REQUIRED

Mass (g) = Molarity (M) × Volume (L) × Molar Mass (g/mol)

Example: Prepare 250 mL of 0.1 M Na₂CO₃ solution

Given: M = 0.1 M, V = 0.25 L, MM of Na₂CO₃ = 106 g/mol

Mass = 0.1 × 0.25 × 106

Mass = 2.65 g

⚖️ WEIGH THE SUBSTANCE

  1. Switch on analytical balance and let it stabilize
  2. Tare the weighing bottle (set to zero)
  3. Using a spatula, add primary standard to weighing bottle
  4. Weigh accurately (to 4 decimal places)
  5. Record the exact mass (e.g., 2.6523 g)
⚠️ Important: Don't try to weigh exactly 2.65g! Weigh close to it and record the exact value you get.

💧 DISSOLVE IN SMALL AMOUNT OF SOLVENT

  1. Transfer the weighed substance to a clean beaker
  2. Add about 50-100 mL of distilled water (or appropriate solvent)
  3. Stir with glass rod until completely dissolved
  4. For difficult-to-dissolve substances, you may:
    • → Heat gently (if substance is heat-stable)
    • → Add acid/base if needed (e.g., CaCO₃ dissolves in HCl)
    • → Stir for longer period

🔄 TRANSFER TO VOLUMETRIC FLASK

  1. Place funnel in neck of volumetric flask (e.g., 250 mL)
  2. Pour the solution from beaker into flask through funnel
  3. Rinse beaker with small portions of distilled water
  4. Transfer all rinsings to the volumetric flask
  5. Rinse the glass rod and funnel into the flask
  6. Make sure NO solution is lost during transfer!

📏 DILUTE TO MARK

  1. Remove funnel from volumetric flask
  2. Add distilled water gradually until nearly at the mark
  3. When solution is about 1 cm below mark:
    • → Use dropper/pipette for final additions
    • → Add water dropwise
  4. Adjust meniscus to touch the calibration mark at eye level
  5. The meniscus bottom should just touch the line
💡 Tip: View the meniscus at eye level to avoid parallax error!

🔀 MIX THOROUGHLY

  1. Stopper the volumetric flask tightly
  2. Invert the flask at least 20-30 times
  3. Ensure complete mixing by swirling
  4. Check that no air bubbles remain

🧮 CALCULATE ACTUAL CONCENTRATION

Since you weighed the exact mass (not exactly what you calculated), calculate the actual concentration:

Actual Molarity = (Actual Mass / Molar Mass) / Volume in Liters

Example: You actually weighed 2.6523 g (not exactly 2.65 g)

Actual M = (2.6523 / 106) / 0.25 = 0.1001 M

✓ Your solution is 0.1001 M (not exactly 0.1 M)

🏷️ LABEL AND STORE

  1. Transfer solution to clean, dry storage bottle
  2. Label clearly with:
    • → Name of substance
    • → Actual concentration
    • → Date of preparation
    • → Your name/initials
  3. Store appropriately:
    • → Cool, dark place for light-sensitive solutions
    • → Tightly sealed to prevent evaporation
    • → Away from incompatible chemicals

⚠️ IMPORTANT PRECAUTIONS

❌ DO NOT:

  • Add water to flask first then add solute
  • Try to weigh exactly the calculated mass
  • Overshoot the calibration mark
  • Use tap water (use distilled/deionized water)
  • Use dirty or wet glassware

✅ DO:

  • Use analytical balance for accurate weighing
  • Record the exact mass weighed
  • Rinse all glassware thoroughly
  • Check meniscus at eye level
  • Mix solution thoroughly after dilution

📝 COMPLETE WORKED EXAMPLE

Problem: Prepare 500 mL of 0.05 M sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) solution

SOLUTION:

Step 1: Calculate mass

Given: M = 0.05 M, V = 0.5 L, MM of Na₂CO₃ = 106 g/mol

Mass = M × V × MM = 0.05 × 0.5 × 106

Mass = 2.65 g

Step 2: Weigh

Actual mass weighed = 2.6487 g (recorded from balance)

Steps 3-6: Dissolve, transfer, dilute, mix

  • Dissolved 2.6487 g in ~100 mL water in beaker
  • Transferred quantitatively to 500 mL volumetric flask
  • Diluted to mark with distilled water
  • Mixed thoroughly by inverting 30 times

Step 7: Calculate actual concentration

Actual M = (2.6487 / 106) / 0.5

Actual M = 0.02499 / 0.5

Actual M = 0.04998 M ≈ 0.0500 M

Step 8: Label

Label: 0.0500 M Na₂CO₃, prepared [date], [your name]

4. 🎯 STANDARDIZATION

📖 DEFINITION

Standardization is the process of determining the exact concentration of a solution by titrating it against a primary standard or another solution of accurately known concentration.

❓ WHY NEEDED?

  • Some reagents cannot be obtained in pure form (e.g., HCl, NaOH)
  • Some solutions change concentration over time
  • To verify concentration of prepared solutions
  • Required for accurate quantitative analysis

THE PROCESS

  1. Prepare solution of approximate concentration
  2. Weigh accurately a primary standard
  3. Dissolve primary standard in solvent
  4. Titrate solution against primary standard
  5. Calculate exact concentration from titration data

📊 EXAMPLE - Standardizing NaOH

  1. Prepare ~0.1M NaOH solution
  2. Accurately weigh 0.5-0.7g potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP)
  3. Dissolve in water
  4. Add phenolphthalein indicator
  5. Titrate with NaOH until pink endpoint
MNaOH = (massKHP / MMKHP) / (VolumeNaOH in L)

📅 FREQUENCY

  • Fresh solutions: immediately after preparation
  • Stored solutions: weekly or before critical analyses
  • After long storage periods
  • When accuracy is questioned

5. 🧮 CONCENTRATION CALCULATIONS

Finding Concentration from Specific Gravity, % Purity, and Molar Mass

📐 MAIN FORMULA

Molarity (M) = (Specific Gravity × % Purity × 10) / Molar Mass

OR

Molarity (M) = (ρ × % Purity × 1000) / Molar Mass

📝 WHERE:

  • ρ (rho) = density = Specific Gravity × 1 g/mL
  • % Purity = percentage as decimal (e.g., 98% = 0.98)
  • Molar Mass = in g/mol
  • 1000 = converts g/mL to g/L

Find density

Density = Specific Gravity × 1 g/mL

Find mass of pure solute per mL

Mass of pure solute = Density × (% Purity/100)

Find mass per liter

Mass per liter = Mass per mL × 1000

Find Molarity

Molarity = Mass per liter / Molar Mass

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE

Given: Concentrated HCl with SG = 1.18, Purity = 37%, MM = 36.5 g/mol

Find: Molarity

Solution:

M = (1.18 × 37 × 10) / 36.5

M = 436.6 / 36.5

M = 11.96 M ≈ 12 M

Therefore, concentrated HCl is approximately 12 M

📚 OTHER COMMON EXAMPLES

Acid/Base SG % Purity MM Molarity
Conc. H₂SO₄ 1.84 98% 98 18.4 M
Conc. HNO₃ 1.42 70% 63 15.8 M
Conc. NH₃ 0.90 25% 17 13.2 M

💧 DILUTION CALCULATION

To prepare dilute solution from concentrated:

M₁V₁ = M₂V₂

Example: Prepare 1L of 0.1M HCl from 12M HCl

V₁ = (0.1 × 1000) / 12 = 8.33 mL

Take 8.33 mL conc. HCl and dilute to 1L

6. 🔴🟦 REDOX TITRATION

📖 DEFINITION

Redox titration is based on oxidation-reduction reactions between the analyte and titrant.

⚡ PRINCIPLE

  • Transfer of electrons between oxidizing and reducing agents
  • Oxidation: loss of electrons (increase in oxidation number)
  • Reduction: gain of electrons (decrease in oxidation number)
  • Equivalence point: moles of electrons lost = moles gained

A) PERMANGANOMETRY

  • Titrant: KMnO₄ (potassium permanganate)
  • Self-indicating (purple color)
  • Medium: acidic (H₂SO₄)
  • Endpoint: faint pink color
  • Half-reaction: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O

B) DICHROMATE METHOD

  • Titrant: K₂Cr₂O₇ (potassium dichromate)
  • Indicator: diphenylamine or ferroin
  • Medium: acidic (H₂SO₄)
  • Half-reaction: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O

C) IODOMETRY (Indirect)

  • Excess I⁻ added to oxidizing agent
  • I₂ liberated is titrated with Na₂S₂O₃
  • Indicator: starch (blue complex with I₂)
  • Used for: Cu²⁺, Cl₂, H₂O₂ determination

D) IODIMETRY (Direct)

  • Direct titration with I₂ solution
  • Used for reducing agents
  • Indicator: starch

🧮 CALCULATIONS

n₁M₁V₁ = n₂M₂V₂

Where n = number of electrons transferred

📊 EXAMPLE

Reaction: KMnO₄ + Fe²⁺ in acid medium

MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O

n(MnO₄⁻) = 5, n(Fe²⁺) = 1

If 25mL of 0.02M KMnO₄ oxidizes Fe²⁺:

Moles Fe²⁺ = 5 × 0.02 × 0.025 = 0.0025 mol

🎯 COMMON APPLICATIONS

  • Determination of Fe²⁺ in iron tablets
  • Assay of H₂O₂ in disinfectants
  • Determination of oxalate in kidney stones
  • Water hardness (sometimes)
  • Vitamin C determination

7. 🧪 STANDARDIZATION OF 0.1M HCl

⚗️ PRINCIPLE

HCl is standardized against anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), a primary standard base.

2HCl + Na₂CO₃ → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂

🛠️ MATERIALS NEEDED

  • Approximately 0.1M HCl solution
  • Anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) - primary standard
  • Methyl orange or bromocresol green indicator
  • Burette, pipette, conical flask
  • Analytical balance

PREPARE HCl SOLUTION

Dilute concentrated HCl to approximately 0.1M using M₁V₁ = M₂V₂

⚖️ WEIGH PRIMARY STANDARD

  • Accurately weigh 0.13-0.15g Na₂CO₃
  • Use analytical balance (4 decimal places)
  • Record exact mass
  • Molar mass Na₂CO₃ = 106 g/mol

💧 DISSOLVE

  • Transfer Na₂CO₃ to conical flask
  • Add 50mL distilled water
  • Swirl to dissolve completely

🎨 ADD INDICATOR

  • Add 2-3 drops of methyl orange indicator
  • Solution turns yellow

📊 TITRATE

  • Fill burette with HCl solution
  • Record initial reading
  • Titrate slowly, swirling constantly
  • Near endpoint, add dropwise
  • Endpoint: yellow → orange → pink
  • Record final reading

🔄 REPEAT

Perform at least 3 concordant titrations (within 0.1 mL of each other)

🧮 CALCULATE

MHCl = (massNa₂CO₃ / MMNa₂CO₃) × (2 / VHCl)

Factor of 2 because 2 moles HCl react with 1 mole Na₂CO₃

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE

Mass Na₂CO₃ = 0.1325 g

Average titre = 25.00 mL = 0.025 L

MHCl = (0.1325 / 106) × (2 / 0.025)

MHCl = 0.00125 × 80

MHCl = 0.100 M

⚠️ PRECAUTIONS

  • ✓ Use freshly boiled and cooled distilled water (to remove CO₂)
  • ✓ Na₂CO₃ must be anhydrous (dry in oven at 270°C if needed)
  • ✓ Titrate slowly near endpoint
  • ✓ Avoid exposure of Na₂CO₃ to air (absorbs moisture and CO₂)

8. 🎨 DOUBLE INDICATOR TITRATION

Double indicator titration uses two indicators to determine two endpoints in the same titration, typically for mixtures of carbonate and hydroxide or carbonate and bicarbonate.

🎯 COMMON APPLICATION

Analysis of Na₂CO₃ + NaOH mixture

STAGE 1: Phenolphthalein indicator

NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O

Na₂CO₃ + HCl → NaHCO₃ + NaCl

Endpoint: Pink → Colorless (pH ≈ 8.3)

STAGE 2: Methyl orange indicator

NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂

Endpoint: Yellow → Orange/Red (pH ≈ 3.7)

🧮 CALCULATIONS

Let V₁ = volume to phenolphthalein endpoint

Let V₂ = additional volume to methyl orange endpoint

CASE 1: Na₂CO₃ + NaOH mixture

Volume for NaOH = V₁ - V₂
Volume for Na₂CO₃ = 2V₂

CASE 2: Na₂CO₃ + NaHCO₃ mixture

Volume for Na₂CO₃ = 2V₁
Volume for NaHCO₃ = V₂ - V₁

CASE 3: Pure Na₂CO₃

V₁ = V₂

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE

Sample containing Na₂CO₃ + NaOH titrated with 0.1M HCl

V₁ (phenolphthalein) = 30 mL

Total volume (methyl orange) = 40 mL

Therefore V₂ = 40 - 30 = 10 mL

Volume for NaOH = V₁ - V₂ = 30 - 10 = 20 mL

Moles NaOH = 0.1 × 0.020 = 0.002 mol

Volume for Na₂CO₃ = 2V₂ = 2 × 10 = 20 mL

Moles Na₂CO₃ = 0.1 × 0.020 = 0.002 mol

9. ↩️ BACK TITRATION

Back titration is a technique where excess reagent is added to the analyte, then the unreacted excess is titrated with a second standard solution.

❓ WHEN USED

  • Analyte is insoluble in water
  • Analyte reacts slowly
  • Analyte is volatile
  • Direct titration endpoint is unclear
  • Analyte is in solid form

🧮 FORMULA

Moles of analyte = Moles of A added - Moles of A remaining

📝 EXAMPLE: CaCO₃ in Antacid Tablets

Reaction: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ (slow reaction)

  1. Dissolve tablet in excess 0.5M HCl (e.g., 50 mL)
  2. Heat to speed up reaction
  3. Cool
  4. Back titrate excess HCl with 0.1M NaOH using phenolphthalein

Calculation:

Moles HCl added = 0.5 × 0.050 = 0.025 mol

Moles HCl reacted with NaOH = 0.1V

Moles HCl reacted with CaCO₃ = 0.025 - 0.1V

Moles CaCO₃ = (0.025 - 0.1V) / 2

10. 🔗 COMPLEXOMETRIC TITRATION

Complexometric titration is based on the formation of colored complexes between metal ions and chelating agents (EDTA is most common).

⚡ PRINCIPLE

  • Metal ions form stable complexes with EDTA
  • Reaction: Mn+ + EDTA⁴⁻ → M-EDTA⁽ⁿ⁻⁴⁾
  • 1:1 stoichiometry regardless of metal charge
  • Uses metallochromic indicators

🎨 MAIN INDICATOR: Eriochrome Black T (EBT)

  • Used for: Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺
  • pH: 8-10 (ammonia buffer)
  • Free indicator: Blue
  • Metal-indicator complex: Wine red
  • Endpoint: Wine red → Blue

🧮 CALCULATION (Water Hardness)

Total hardness (mg/L as CaCO₃) = (MEDTA × VEDTA × 100,000) / Vsample

🎯 APPLICATIONS

  • Water hardness determination (Total, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺)
  • Calcium in milk
  • Zinc in pharmaceuticals
  • Lead, copper, nickel in samples
  • Metal content in alloys

11. ⚖️ MILLIEQUIVALENT CALCULATIONS

A milliequivalent (mEq) is 1/1000 of an equivalent. Commonly used in clinical chemistry and pharmaceutical calculations.

📐 FORMULAS

mEq = (mass in mg / molecular weight) × valence
mEq = mmol × valence
mg = (mEq × MW) / valence

📝 EXAMPLES

Example 1: Calculate mEq in 234 mg of NaCl

MW of NaCl = 58.5 g/mol, Valence = 1

mEq = (234 / 58.5) × 1 = 4 mEq

Example 2: How many mg in 5 mEq of CaCl₂?

MW of CaCl₂ = 111 g/mol, Valence of Ca²⁺ = 2

mg = (5 × 111) / 2 = 277.5 mg

💉 CLINICAL APPLICATIONS

Normal serum levels:

  • Na⁺: 135-145 mEq/L
  • K⁺: 3.5-5.0 mEq/L
  • Ca²⁺: 4.5-5.5 mEq/L
  • Cl⁻: 95-105 mEq/L

12. 🧬 EDTA - FULL MEANING

📖 FULL NAME

Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid

📋 ALTERNATIVE NAMES

  • Edetic acid
  • Ethylenediamine-N,N,N',N'-tetraacetic acid
  • EDTA-4Na (tetrasodium salt)
  • EDTA-2Na (disodium salt) - most common form

🧪 CHEMICAL FORMULA

  • Free acid: C₁₀H₁₆N₂O₈
  • Disodium salt: C₁₀H₁₄N₂Na₂O₈·2H₂O
  • Molecular weight: 372.24 g/mol (disodium salt dihydrate)

⭐ KEY FEATURES

  • Hexadentate ligand (6 donor atoms)
  • 4 carboxyl groups (-COOH)
  • 2 amine groups (=N-)
  • Forms cage-like structure around metal ions
  • Very stable complexes (chelates)
  • 1:1 stoichiometry with metal ions

🎯 USES

In Analytical Chemistry:

  • Complexometric titrations
  • Metal ion determination
  • Water hardness testing
  • Masking agent

Other Uses:

  • Food preservative (antioxidant)
  • Chelation therapy (heavy metal poisoning)
  • Cosmetics and personal care products
  • Blood anticoagulant

13. 🎯 ACCURACY vs PRECISION

ACCURACY

How close to TRUE value

  • Measures correctness
  • Closeness to true/accepted value
  • Related to systematic error
  • One measurement can be accurate
  • Example: hitting bullseye

PRECISION

How close to EACH OTHER

  • Measures reproducibility
  • Closeness to each other
  • Related to random error
  • Need multiple measurements
  • Example: tight grouping

🎯 DARTBOARD ANALOGY

Situation Description
High Accuracy, High Precision 🎯 All darts clustered tightly at bullseye (IDEAL)
High Accuracy, Low Precision Darts scattered around bullseye (average correct)
Low Accuracy, High Precision Darts tightly clustered away from bullseye (systematic error)
Low Accuracy, Low Precision Darts scattered far from bullseye (worst case)

🧮 QUANTIFYING

Accuracy:

% error = [(Measured - True) / True] × 100%

Precision:

Standard deviation (SD)
Coefficient of variation (CV) = (SD / Mean) × 100%

💡 You can have precision without accuracy, but accuracy without precision suggests you were just lucky!

14. ⭐ PRIMARY STANDARDS

A primary standard is a highly purified, stable compound used to prepare standard solutions of accurately known concentration or to standardize other solutions.

✅ CHARACTERISTICS (10 Requirements)

  1. High Purity (>99.9%) - Available in very pure form
  2. Stability - Does not decompose on storage
  3. Large Molecular Weight - Reduces weighing errors
  4. Non-Hygroscopic - Does not absorb moisture from air
  5. Readily Available - Commercially available, reasonably priced
  6. Easy to Dry - Can be dried to constant weight
  7. Soluble - Readily soluble in solvent used
  8. High Equivalent Weight - Reduces relative error
  9. Stoichiometric Reaction - Complete, known reaction
  10. Non-Toxic (preferable) - Safe to handle

🧪 COMMON PRIMARY STANDARDS

Primary Standard Used For Indicator
Na₂CO₃ HCl / H₂SO₄ standardization Methyl orange
KHP (Potassium Hydrogen Phthalate) NaOH / KOH standardization Phenolphthalein
K₂Cr₂O₇ Na₂S₂O₃ standardization Various
Na₂C₂O₄ KMnO₄ standardization Self-indicating
CaCO₃ EDTA standardization EBT

❌ NOT PRIMARY STANDARDS

  • NaOH: Hygroscopic, absorbs CO₂
  • HCl: Volatile, concentration changes
  • KMnO₄: Not high purity, decomposes in light
  • Na₂S₂O₃: Hydrated, decomposes slowly
  • I₂: Volatile, solutions unstable

→ These must all be standardized!
